ITIL 4 Foundation

A structured, exam-accurate study guide based on the official ITIL 4 Foundation publication (AXELOS). All definitions, terminology, and concepts are sourced directly from the official syllabus.

Key Definitions — Start Here

Service (Official Definition) A means of enabling value co-creation by facilitating outcomes that customers want to achieve, without the customer having to manage specific costs and risks.
Value (Official Definition) The perceived benefits, usefulness, and importance of something. Value is co-created through an active collaboration between providers and consumers, as well as other organisations that are part of the relevant service relationships.
Service Management (Official Definition) A set of specialised organisational capabilities for enabling value for customers in the form of services.
IT Service Management (ITSM) The implementation and management of quality IT services that meet the needs of the business. ITSM is performed by IT service providers through an appropriate mix of people, process, and information technology.

Utility and Warranty — Critical Distinction

Both are required for a service to deliver value. This is a frequent exam topic.

Utility — "Fit for Purpose" The functionality offered by a product or service to meet a particular need. Utility can be summarised as what the service does. A service must either support the performance of the consumer or remove constraints from the consumer to be considered to have utility.
Warranty — "Fit for Use" Assurance that a product or service will meet agreed requirements. Warranty relates to how the service performs. It typically addresses areas such as availability, capacity, security levels, and continuity. A service can have utility without warranty and will not deliver value.

Stakeholder Types

Official definition: A person who defines the requirements for a service and takes responsibility for the outcomes of service consumption.

Customers may be internal (within the same organisation as the provider) or external. The customer is responsible for defining what constitutes value and authorising the budget for the service.

Exam tip: The customer defines requirements and pays — they are not necessarily the day-to-day user of the service.

Official definition: A person who uses services.

Users interact with the service on a day-to-day basis. A user and a customer can be the same person (e.g. a sole trader who procures and uses cloud storage) or different (e.g. employees who use IT services that their employer has procured).

Official definition: A person who authorises budget for service consumption.

In practice, the sponsor, customer, and user may all be the same person, or they can be distinct roles. In large organisations they are usually separate — a CFO (sponsor) approves budgets, a department head (customer) defines requirements, and employees (users) use the service daily.

Official definition: A role performed by an organisation in a service relationship to provide services to consumers.

Providers can be internal (shared services, IT department) or external (cloud provider, managed service provider). An organisation may simultaneously be a provider to some parties and a consumer of others.

Outputs, Outcomes, Costs, and Risks

Output A tangible or intangible deliverable of an activity. Example: a report, an application, a resolved ticket.
Outcome A result for a stakeholder enabled by one or more outputs. Example: the business is able to process orders faster because the application was deployed. Outcomes are what the customer actually cares about.
Cost The amount of money spent on a specific activity or resource. Costs can be removed from the consumer (a benefit) or imposed on the consumer (a constraint). Service providers absorb costs consumers do not want to manage themselves.
Risk A possible event that could cause harm or loss, or make it more difficult to achieve objectives. Risks can be removed from the consumer (a benefit) or imposed on the consumer (a constraint). Consumers accept some risks in exchange for the value a service delivers.

Service Value System

The ITIL 4 Service Value System (SVS) describes how all the components and activities of an organisation work together as a system to enable value creation. The SVS has inputs — opportunity and demand — and outputs — value.

Official SVS Definition The ITIL SVS describes how all the components and activities of an organisation work together as a system to enable value creation. Each organisation's SVS is connected to other organisations, forming ecosystems which can in turn enable value for those organisations, their customers, and other stakeholders.

The 5 Components of the SVS

Recommendations that guide an organisation in all circumstances, regardless of changes to its goals, strategies, type of work, or management structure. The guiding principles are universal and enduring.

There are 7 guiding principles. They are not prescriptive rules — they are considered recommendations. An organisation should consider which principles are relevant and how to apply them in a given situation. See the Principles tab for full detail.

Official definition: The means by which an organisation is directed and controlled.

Governance includes the activities of Evaluate, Direct, and Monitor (EDM):

  • Evaluate: The governing body evaluates the organisation, its strategy, its portfolios, and its relationships with other parties.
  • Direct: The governing body assigns responsibility for, and directs preparation and implementation of, strategy and policies.
  • Monitor: The governing body monitors performance of the organisation and its practices, products, and services.

Exam tip: Governance sits above management. The governing body (e.g. board of directors) evaluates, directs, and monitors — it does not do the day-to-day work.

An operating model that outlines the key activities required to respond to demand and facilitate value realisation through the creation and management of products and services.

The SVC has six activities. They are not a linear process — they combine flexibly into value streams that define a specific sequence of activities for a given scenario. See the Value Chain tab for full detail.

Official definition of a Practice: A set of organisational resources designed for performing work or accomplishing an objective.

ITIL 4 includes 34 management practices grouped into three categories:

  • 14 General Management Practices — adopted and adapted from general business management domains
  • 17 Service Management Practices — developed in IT service management and ITSM communities
  • 3 Technical Management Practices — adapted from technology management domains

Note: ITIL 4 uses the term "practice" rather than "process." A practice includes processes, but also people, tools, information, skills, and other resources.

A recurring organisational activity performed at all levels to ensure that an organisation's performance continually meets stakeholders' expectations. Continual Improvement applies to the SVS as a whole — its components, as well as to the organisation's products, services, and relationships.

ITIL 4 provides the Continual Improvement Model (7 steps) to structure improvement initiatives:

  • 1. What is the vision?
  • 2. Where are we now?
  • 3. Where do we want to be?
  • 4. How do we get there?
  • 5. Take action
  • 6. Did we get there?
  • 7. How do we keep the momentum going?

Exam tip: Continual Improvement is both an SVS component AND one of the 34 management practices. This dual role is deliberate — it is a practice that is also embedded in the overall system.

Inputs and Outputs of the SVS

Inputs Opportunity: Options or possibilities to add value for stakeholders or otherwise improve the organisation.

Demand: Need or desire for products and services from internal and external customers.
Output Value: The perceived benefits, usefulness, and importance of something. Value is always determined by the consumer, not the provider.

Organisational Agility and the SVS

The SVS explicitly supports many work approaches including Agile, DevOps, and Lean. ITIL 4 does not conflict with these — it is designed to integrate with them. The concept of organisational agility is the ability of an organisation to move and adapt quickly, flexibly, and decisively.

Service Value Chain

The Service Value Chain (SVC) is an operating model that outlines the key activities required to respond to demand and facilitate value realisation. It sits at the heart of the SVS.

Value Stream (Official Definition) A series of steps an organisation undertakes to create and deliver products and services to a service consumer. A value stream is a combination of the organisation's activities in the service value chain.

The six SVC activities are not a linear sequence. They interconnect and combine in different orders depending on the scenario. Each activity transforms inputs into outputs using various practices.

The Six Activities

Purpose: To ensure a shared understanding of the vision, current status, and improvement direction for all four dimensions and all products and services across the organisation.

Key inputs: Policies and requirements from governance, demand and opportunities, value chain performance information, improvement initiatives and plans, improvement status reports.

Key outputs: Strategic, tactical, and operational plans; portfolio decisions; architectures and policies; improvement opportunities.

Exam tip: Plan feeds all other SVC activities. It ensures alignment between the overall SVS direction and what each activity is working toward.

Purpose: To ensure continual improvement of products, services, and practices across all value chain activities and the four dimensions of service management.

Key inputs: Product and service performance information, stakeholders' feedback, performance information and improvement opportunities from all SVC activities.

Key outputs: Improvement initiatives and plans, value chain and practice performance information, improvement status reports, contract and agreement requirements.

Exam tip: Improve is also present in every other SVC activity — continual improvement is not a separate phase, it runs in parallel with everything else.

Purpose: To provide a good understanding of stakeholder needs, transparency, and continual engagement and good relationships with all stakeholders.

Key inputs: Product and service portfolio (provided by Plan), high-level demand for services and products (from customers), detailed requirements (from customers), requests and feedback from customers/users.

Key outputs: Consolidated demands and opportunities (to Plan), requirements (to Design & Transition and Obtain/Build), user support tasks (to Deliver & Support), contracts and agreements (to Obtain/Build).

Exam tip: Engage is the primary interface between the SVS and external stakeholders — customers, users, and partners. It is how demand enters the SVC.

Purpose: To ensure that products and services continually meet stakeholder expectations for quality, costs, and time to market.

Key inputs: Portfolio decisions and architectures (from Plan), requirements (from Engage), improvement initiatives (from Improve), knowledge and information about new/changed products from Obtain/Build.

Key outputs: Requirements and specifications (to Obtain/Build), contract and agreement requirements (to Engage), new and changed products and services (to Deliver & Support).

Exam tip: Design & Transition covers both the design of services AND their transition into live operation. In ITIL v3 these were separate phases; in ITIL 4 they are combined into one SVC activity.

Purpose: To ensure that service components are available when and where they are needed, and meet agreed specifications.

Key inputs: Architectures and policies (from Plan), contracts and agreements with internal and external suppliers (from Engage), requirements and specifications (from Design & Transition), improvement initiatives (from Improve).

Key outputs: Service components (to Deliver & Support and Design & Transition), knowledge and information about new/changed service components (to all activities), contract and agreement requirements (to Engage).

Exam tip: Obtain/Build handles the acquisition (buy) or creation (build) of service components. This is where development work and supplier procurement sit.

Purpose: To ensure that services are delivered and supported according to agreed specifications and stakeholders' expectations.

Key inputs: New and changed products and services (from Design & Transition), service components (from Obtain/Build), improvement initiatives (from Improve), user support tasks (from Engage), knowledge and information about new/changed service components (from Obtain/Build).

Key outputs: Services delivered to customers and users, information on the completion of user support tasks (to Engage), product and service performance information (to Engage and Improve), improvement opportunities (to Improve), contract and agreement requirements (to Engage).

Exam tip: This is the activity most associated with the Service Desk, Incident Management, and day-to-day support operations.

34 Management Practices

A practice is a set of organisational resources designed for performing work or accomplishing an objective. The ITIL 4 Foundation exam focuses on 18 of the 34 practices. The most heavily examined are marked ★.

General Management Practices (14)

Adopted and adapted from general business management domains for use in service management environments.

Purpose: To align the organisation's practices and services with changing business needs through the ongoing identification and improvement of services, service components, practices, or any element involved in the efficient and effective management of products and services.

Scope: Applies at all levels — strategic, tactical, and operational. Every person and team is responsible for contributing to continual improvement.

Continual Improvement Register (CIR): A database or document used to track and manage improvement ideas from identification through to final action. All improvement ideas should be logged in the CIR.

Exam tip: "Continual" not "Continuous" — it is deliberate and structured, not constant and uninterrupted.

Purpose: To protect the information needed by the organisation to conduct its business. This includes understanding and managing risks to the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information, as well as other aspects such as authentication and non-repudiation.

Key concepts:

  • Confidentiality: Information is not made available to unauthorised individuals or processes.
  • Integrity: Information is complete and accurate and protected from unauthorised modification.
  • Availability: Information is accessible and usable when required.
  • Authentication: Users are who they say they are.
  • Non-repudiation: A party cannot deny the authenticity of their signature on a document.

Purpose: To establish and nurture the links between the organisation and its stakeholders at strategic and tactical levels. It identifies, analyses, monitors, and continually improves the relationships with and between stakeholders.

Key output: Customer engagement — building long-term, mutually beneficial relationships rather than transactional exchanges.

Purpose: To ensure that the organisation's suppliers and their performances are managed appropriately to support the seamless provision of quality products and services. This includes creating closer, more collaborative relationships with key suppliers to uncover and realise new value and reduce the risk of failure.

Supplier categorisation: Suppliers may be categorised by spend, strategic importance, or risk — determining the level of management and oversight applied.

Underpinning Contract (UC): A formal agreement with an external supplier that underpins an SLA. Managed by Supplier Management.

  • Architecture Management: Understanding all elements in an organisation and how they interrelate, enabling the organisation to achieve current and future objectives.
  • Knowledge Management: Maintaining and improving the effective, efficient, and convenient use of information and knowledge across the organisation. Key tool: the SKMS (Service Knowledge Management System).
  • Measurement & Reporting: Supports good decision-making and continual improvement by decreasing levels of uncertainty. Metrics must support desired outcomes, not just activity.
  • Organisational Change Management: Ensures changes in an organisation are implemented smoothly and successfully, and lasting benefits are achieved. Addresses the human aspects of change.
  • Portfolio Management: Ensures the organisation has the right mix of programmes, projects, products, and services to execute its strategy within funding and resource constraints.
  • Project Management: Ensures all projects are successfully delivered. Includes planning, delegation, monitoring, and control of all aspects of a project.
  • Risk Management: Ensures the organisation understands and effectively handles risks. Value, outcomes, costs, and risks are at the heart of ITIL 4's approach.
  • Service Financial Management: Supports the organisation's strategies and plans for service management by ensuring that the financial aspects of services, products, and components are understood and managed.
  • Strategy Management: Formulates the goals of the organisation and adopts courses of action and allocation of resources necessary to achieve those goals.
  • Workforce & Talent Management: Ensures the organisation has the right people with the appropriate skills and knowledge in the correct roles to support business objectives.

Service Management Practices (17)

Developed in IT service management and ITSM communities. These are the most heavily tested in the Foundation exam.

Purpose: To maximise the number of successful IT changes by ensuring that risks have been properly assessed, authorising changes to proceed, and managing the change schedule.

Note: ITIL 4 renamed this from "Change Management" to "Change Enablement" to reflect the shift toward enabling change rather than controlling it.

Change: The addition, modification, or removal of anything that could have a direct or indirect effect on services.

Change Authority: A person or group responsible for authorising a change. The authority level depends on the type and risk of change.

Change Schedule: A document that shows the planned changes and their proposed implementation dates, as well as the current status of changes.

Three Change Types:

  • Standard change: A change to a service or other CI where the approach is pre-authorised by change authority, is low risk, is relatively common, and follows a procedure or work instruction. Standard changes do not need individual assessment each time.
  • Normal change: A change that is not a standard change or an emergency change. Assessed via the normal change management process and requires authorisation before implementation. May be reviewed by a Change Advisory Board (CAB).
  • Emergency change: A change that must be implemented as soon as possible; for example, to resolve an incident or implement a security patch. The assessment and authorisation process is expedited. May be reviewed by an Emergency Change Advisory Board (ECAB).

CAB (Change Advisory Board): A group of people that supports the assessment, prioritisation, authorisation, and scheduling of changes. Not all changes go to a CAB — only those where its involvement adds value.

Purpose: To minimise the negative impact of incidents by restoring normal service operation as quickly as possible.

Incident: An unplanned interruption to a service or reduction in the quality of a service.

Major Incident: An incident with significant business impact, requiring an immediate coordinated resolution. A separate procedure is used for major incidents, with shorter timescales and greater urgency.

Key activities:

  • Logging and classifying incidents
  • Prioritising based on impact and urgency
  • Investigating and diagnosing
  • Resolving and recovering
  • Closing the incident

Exam tip: Incident Management restores service — it does not necessarily fix the underlying cause. Fixing root causes is Problem Management's responsibility.

Workaround: A solution that reduces or eliminates the impact of an incident or problem for which a full resolution is not yet available.

Purpose: To reduce the likelihood and impact of incidents by identifying actual and potential causes of incidents, and managing workarounds and known errors.

Problem: A cause, or potential cause, of one or more incidents.

Known Error: A problem that has been analysed and has not been resolved.

Three phases of Problem Management:

  • Problem identification: Identifying problems reactively (from recurring incidents) and proactively (before incidents occur).
  • Problem control: Analysing problems and documenting workarounds and known errors.
  • Error control: Managing known errors. Regularly reassessing the status of known errors, assessing the impact of the workaround, and deciding when to raise a change request to resolve the problem permanently.

Exam tip: Problems and incidents have separate lifecycles. A problem can remain open while related incidents are closed. Problems are not always resolved — sometimes a workaround is sufficient.

Purpose: To capture demand for incident resolution and service requests. It should also be the entry point and single point of contact for the service provider with all its users.

Note: The Service Desk is a practice, not just a team or tool.

Service Desk structures:

  • Local: Co-located with users. Good for high-touch or specialised support, but expensive to run at scale.
  • Centralised: A single service desk serving the whole organisation. More efficient and consistent.
  • Virtual: Staff located in multiple locations but presenting as a single entity using technology. Enables follow-the-sun support.
  • Follow-the-sun: Combines teams from different time zones to provide 24-hour coverage.

Skills required: Empathy, emotional intelligence, incident analysis, effective communication, understanding of business priorities, collaboration skills.

Exam tip: The Service Desk is the single point of contact (SPOC) for users. It logs both incidents and service requests.

Purpose: To set clear business-based targets for service levels, and to ensure that delivery of services is properly assessed, monitored, and managed against these targets.

Service Level Agreement (SLA): A documented agreement between a service provider and a customer that identifies both services required and the expected level of service. SLAs should be written in business terms, not technical jargon.

Operational Level Agreement (OLA): An agreement between a service provider and another part of the same organisation that assists with the provision of services. (Internal.)

Underpinning Contract (UC): A legally binding agreement between a service provider and a third-party supplier. (External.)

Watermelon SLA: An SLA that appears green on the outside (metrics are met) but is red on the inside (customers are unhappy). This occurs when metrics don't reflect actual customer experience. Service Level Management should use both quantitative metrics and qualitative feedback.

Purpose: To support the agreed quality of a service by handling all predefined, user-initiated service requests in an effective and user-friendly manner.

Service request: A request from a user or a user's authorised representative that initiates a service action which has been agreed as a normal part of service delivery.

Examples: request for a new laptop, password reset, request for information, request for a standard change.

Exam tip: Service requests are not incidents. They are pre-approved and do not represent a failure. They should be fulfilled via known, documented procedures.

Key principle: Service requests should be fulfilled as efficiently as possible. Fulfilment steps should be standardised and automated where possible to maximise efficiency.

Purpose: To systematically observe services and service components, and record and report selected changes of state identified as events.

Event: Any change of state that has significance for the management of a service or other CI.

Three event types:

  • Informational: Does not require action. Recorded for reference (e.g. a user successfully logged in).
  • Warning: Indicates a threshold is approaching or unusual behaviour is occurring. May need attention before it becomes an incident.
  • Exception: Indicates that normal operation has been breached. Typically triggers incident management. (e.g. a server is down.)

Purpose: To plan and manage the full lifecycle of all IT assets, to help the organisation maximise value, control costs, manage risks, support decision-making about purchase, re-use, retirement, and disposal of assets, and meet regulatory and contractual requirements.

IT Asset: Any financially valuable component that can contribute to the delivery of an IT product or service.

Exam tip: IT Asset Management manages the financial and contractual aspects of assets throughout their lifecycle. It differs from Service Configuration Management, which focuses on the relationships between CIs.

Purpose: To ensure that accurate and reliable information about the configuration of services, and the CIs that support them, is available when and where it is needed.

Configuration Item (CI): Any component that needs to be managed in order to deliver an IT service. CIs may include hardware, software, networks, buildings, people, and formal documentation.

Configuration Management Database (CMDB): A database used to store configuration records throughout their lifecycle. The CMDB also maintains the relationships between CIs.

Configuration Management System (CMS): A set of tools, data, and information used to support Service Configuration Management. The CMS may include one or more CMDBs.

Configuration Record: A record containing the details of a CI. Each CI should have a configuration record.

Purpose: To make new and changed services and features available for use.

Release: A version of a service or other CI, or a collection of CIs, that is made available for use.

Exam tip — Release vs Deployment: These are distinct practices in ITIL 4.

  • Release Management decides when and what is made available to customers/users.
  • Deployment Management physically moves components into live environments.

A release may involve multiple deployments; not every deployment results in a visible release.

  • Availability Management: Ensuring services deliver agreed levels of availability to meet customer needs. Measures availability as a percentage of agreed service time.
  • Business Analysis: Analysing business and its needs, and facilitating appropriate solutions to problems.
  • Capacity and Performance Management: Ensuring services achieve agreed and expected performance, satisfying current and future demand in a cost-effective way.
  • Service Catalogue Management: Provides a single source of consistent information on all services and service offerings available from the provider.
  • Service Continuity Management: Ensuring availability and performance of a service is maintained at sufficient levels in the event of a disaster (BCDR focus).
  • Service Design: Designing products and services that are fit for purpose, fit for use, and can be delivered by the organisation and its ecosystem of partners and suppliers.
  • Service Validation and Testing: Ensuring that new or changed products and services meet defined requirements.

Technical Management Practices (3)

Purpose: To move new or changed hardware, software, documentation, processes, or any other component to live environments. May also be involved in deploying to other environments for testing or staging.

Deployment Management works closely with Release Management and Change Enablement but is technically distinct from both.

Purpose: To oversee the infrastructure and platforms used by an organisation. This enables the monitoring of technology solutions available, including solutions offered by third parties.

Purpose: To ensure that applications meet internal and external stakeholder needs, in terms of functionality, reliability, maintainability, compliance, and auditability.

7 Guiding Principles

Guiding principles are recommendations that can guide an organisation in all circumstances, regardless of changes to its goals, strategies, type of work, or management structure. A guiding principle is universal and enduring.

Important Exam Point Guiding principles are recommendations, not rules. An organisation should consider the relevance of each principle and how it applies to a given situation. Multiple principles may apply simultaneously and should be considered together. No principle stands alone.

Everything the organisation does should link back, directly or indirectly, to value for itself, its customers, and other stakeholders.

When adopting this principle, consider:

  • Know how service consumers use each service and what the service helps them achieve.
  • Encourage a focus on value among all staff, even those not directly facing customers.
  • Value is defined by the consumer, not the provider. Always ask: what does value mean to this stakeholder?
  • Focus on value during every step of improvement initiatives — not just at the start.

Common mistake: Teams focus on output (what they deliver) rather than outcomes (what customers achieve). This principle addresses that gap.

Do not start from scratch and build something new without considering what is already available to be leveraged.

When adopting this principle, consider:

  • Look at what exists as objectively as possible using direct observation before deciding what to build or change.
  • When examples of successful practices are found in the current state, determine if these can be expanded to a wider scale.
  • Apply risk management skills: the current state may contain the seeds of a better state.
  • Recognise that measurement can distort what is seen. Assess what you observe, not just what is measured.

Resist the temptation to do everything at once. Even huge initiatives must be accomplished iteratively.

When adopting this principle, consider:

  • Comprehend the whole but do something: understand the big picture but begin improvement through manageable iterations.
  • Use feedback before, throughout, and after each iteration to ensure actions are focused and appropriate.
  • Fast does not mean incomplete: iterations can be very short or longer, but must include a defined outcome and build toward the overall objective.

Link to Agile: This principle reflects Agile and DevOps thinking — short feedback loops, minimum viable products, and incremental delivery.

Working together across boundaries produces results that have greater buy-in, more relevance to objectives, and increased likelihood of long-term success.

When adopting this principle, consider:

  • Collaboration does not mean consensus: not every decision requires full agreement from all parties, but people need to feel heard.
  • Communicate in a way the audience can hear: tailor communication style to the audience.
  • Decisions cannot be made on invisible data: insufficient visibility of work leads to poor decisions, increased risk, and poor outcomes.
  • Identify and manage hidden work — work that exists but is not captured in any system or plan.

No service or element used to provide a service stands alone. The outcomes achieved will suffer unless the organisation works in an integrated way to handle its activities as a whole.

When adopting this principle, consider:

  • Recognise the complexity of systems: a change in one area creates effects in others, sometimes unexpectedly.
  • Collaboration is needed to work holistically — this principle reinforces Principle 4.
  • Where possible, look for patterns in the needs of and interactions between system elements.
  • Automation can facilitate working holistically by connecting systems and reducing manual handoffs.

Link to 4 Dimensions: Holistic thinking requires considering all four dimensions for every service and practice.

Always use the minimum number of steps necessary to accomplish an objective.

When adopting this principle, consider:

  • Ensure value: if a process, service, action, or metric fails to provide value or produce a useful outcome, eliminate it.
  • Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication: do not attempt to create a solution for every exception.
  • Do fewer things, but do them better: focus on outcomes, not activity.
  • Respect the time of the people involved: complex processes frustrate those who must follow them.

Common trap: Over-engineering processes. ITIL 4 explicitly discourages adding steps, approvals, or documentation unless they demonstrably add value.

Organisations should maximise the value of the work carried out by their human and technical resources.

When adopting this principle, consider:

  • Optimise first, then automate: Technology should be used to automate tasks only after the underlying process or procedure has been optimised. Automating a broken process makes the problem happen faster.
  • Use the other guiding principles when optimising: Focus on Value, Start Where You Are, Progress Iteratively with Feedback.
  • Automation should be used where human intervention adds no value — freeing humans to focus on higher-value work.
  • Be aware of the limits of automation: not everything should be automated.

Four Dimensions of Service Management

ITIL 4 identifies four dimensions that collectively are critical to the effective and efficient facilitation of value for customers and other stakeholders. All four dimensions must be considered for every service and every element of the SVS.

Official Framing For services to be delivered and managed effectively, they must be considered from all four dimensions. Failure to adequately address any of the four dimensions leads to services that are undeliverable, or that do not meet expectations of quality or efficiency.
PESTLE — External Factors Affecting All Dimensions All four dimensions are influenced by external factors: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, and Environmental. These factors are largely outside the control of the SVS but must be accounted for when designing and improving services.

The Four Dimensions

This dimension encompasses the roles and responsibilities, formal organisational structures, culture, and required staffing and competencies — all of which are related to the creation, delivery, and improvement of a service.

Key considerations:

  • The organisation must ensure that its structure and management supports its overall strategy and operating model.
  • Roles and responsibilities must be clear and understood.
  • The organisation's culture should support its objectives — values, attitudes, and behaviours that are demonstrated by leadership.
  • Everyone in the organisation should understand their contribution to creating value.

Exam tip: Culture is part of this dimension. A technically perfect process will fail without the right organisational culture and behaviours.

This dimension applies to both the management of services and the services themselves. It includes the information and knowledge used to deliver services, as well as the technologies required.

For service management, this includes:

  • Service management information systems (ITSM tools, CMDB, SKMS)
  • Communication systems, workflow engines, analytics
  • Artificial intelligence and machine learning
  • Cloud and mobile platforms

Key questions for this dimension:

  • What information is managed by the services?
  • What information and knowledge is needed to deliver the services?
  • How will the information and knowledge assets be protected, managed, archived, and disposed of?

Exam tip: This dimension also includes information security considerations — confidentiality, integrity, and availability of data.

This dimension encompasses an organisation's relationships with other organisations that are involved in the design, development, deployment, delivery, support, and/or continual improvement of services.

Types of relationships:

  • Goods providers — hardware, software, other products
  • Service providers — managed services, cloud services, outsourcing
  • Partners — closer strategic relationships with mutual goals

The Service Integration and Management (SIAM) approach addresses how to manage multiple suppliers who all contribute to a single service ecosystem.

Exam tip: Organisations must decide which activities to perform internally and which to source externally, based on strategy, risk, cost, and capability.

This dimension defines the activities, workflows, controls, and procedures needed to achieve agreed objectives. It is concerned with how the various parts of the organisation work in an integrated and coordinated way to enable value creation through products and services.

Value stream: A series of steps an organisation undertakes to create and deliver products and services to consumers. Value streams should be designed with the customer's perspective in mind.

Process: A set of interrelated or interacting activities that transform inputs into outputs. Processes define the sequence and dependencies of activities, roles, responsibilities, and controls.

Exam tip: ITIL 4 replaces "processes" with "practices" at the top level, but processes still exist within practices. This dimension specifically focuses on how processes and value streams are designed and operated.

OutputA tangible or intangible deliverable of an activity.
RiskA possible event that could cause harm or loss, or make it harder to achieve objectives.
CostAmount of money spent on a specific activity or resource.

Incidents, Problems, Events

IncidentAn unplanned interruption to a service or reduction in the quality of a service.
Major IncidentAn incident with significant business impact, requiring an immediate coordinated resolution. Uses a separate, expedited procedure.
ProblemA cause, or potential cause, of one or more incidents.
Known ErrorA problem that has been analysed and has not been resolved.
WorkaroundA solution that reduces or eliminates the impact of an incident or problem for which a full resolution is not yet available.
EventAny change of state that has significance for the management of a service or other CI.
Service RequestA request from a user for something that is a normal part of service delivery (not an incident).

Change Types

Standard ChangePre-authorised. Low risk. Follows a documented procedure. No individual assessment needed each time.
Normal ChangeAssessed through the standard change management process. Requires authorisation before implementation. May go to CAB.
Emergency ChangeMust be implemented urgently. Expedited assessment and authorisation. May go to ECAB.

Agreements and Contracts

SLAService Level Agreement. Between service provider and customer. Defines agreed service levels.
OLAOperational Level Agreement. Between the service provider and another internal team. Underpins an SLA.
UCUnderpinning Contract. Legally binding agreement between service provider and external supplier.

Configuration and Assets

CIConfiguration Item. Any component managed to deliver an IT service.
CMDBConfiguration Management Database. Stores configuration records and relationships between CIs.
CMSConfiguration Management System. Broader set of tools that may include multiple CMDBs.
SKMSService Knowledge Management System. The complete set of tools and databases for managing knowledge.
IT AssetAny financially valuable component that can contribute to the delivery of an IT product or service.
